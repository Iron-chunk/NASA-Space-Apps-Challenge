      What the data says about spacecraft fire safety

      Findings reported in NASA's Researcher's Guide to ISS Combustion Science (2015) and the PSI records. Page numbers refer to that guide.

      Lower flammability limit in low gravity

      FLEX found the flammability limit is lower in reduced gravity, so terrestrial standards for inert-gas suppressants do not give the same margin of safety in space.

      Guide p.12 · PSI-69, PSI-68

      Ventilation can make fires worse

      With adequate ventilation, materials burn as well as or better than in normal gravity. In low-speed flow, flames can spread upstream into the oncoming air, the opposite of Earth.

      Guide pp.18, 21 · PSI-26, PSI-25

      Nitrogen can strengthen a flame

      Early BASS results showed a nitrogen jet applied to a flame can strengthen it by entraining oxygen, depending on flame geometry and where the jet hits.

      Guide p.22 · PSI-26

      Smoke particles differ in space

      Particle sizes in microgravity flames differ greatly from those on Earth, so detectors tuned to Earth signatures could be “looking” for the wrong thing. SAME measured smoke particle size distributions from spacecraft materials.

      Guide pp.26, 35 · PSI-102, PSI-101

      Cool flames burn without being seen

      FLEX showed a cool flame can keep burning for a prolonged period after the visible flame goes out, relevant to flame-based detection.

      Guide p.34 · PSI-69, PSI-117, PSI-142

      Water mist replaces CO₂ extinguishers

      The first two of nine planned water-mist portable fire extinguishers went to the ISS in December 2015 to replace CO₂ units.

      Guide p.26

      Three misconceptions the research corrected

      1. A steady fire cannot exist in a quiescent microgravity environment.
      2. A fire will self-extinguish if ventilation is shut off.
      3. Because reduced gravity is inherently safer, terrestrial fire standards and tests are very conservative.

      Guide p.35

      Why it matters on Earth

      NASA fire-safety work has a track record of reaching firefighters, mines and homes. The records add combustion models and detection data to that line.

      Smoke and dust discrimination

      The Brunswick Incipient Fire Detector, used in space shuttle orbiters, tells combustion particles from dust, which cuts false alarms and suits large areas such as mines (1978).

      NASA Spinoff · Fire Detector

      Firefighter air tanks

      Spacesuit technology led to a 20 lb backpack with a 30-minute air supply, 13 lb lighter than conventional tanks, pressurized at 4,500 psi (1976).

      NASA Spinoff · Fireman's Air Tanks

      Protective clothing

      Project FIRES applied lightweight, heat-protective materials from Apollo-era spacesuits to firefighter gear, tested by fourteen municipal fire departments (1982).

      NASA Spinoff · New Technology for the Fire Attire

      Cleaner, safer combustion

      BASS-II results feed models used to design fire detection and suppression systems in microgravity and on Earth. SPICE-based soot work (PSI-60) supports safer detection and cleaner combustion.

      PSI-25, PSI-60

      Why the work exists

      Three fires set the agenda. In each, an oxygen-rich atmosphere or oxygen source was part of the story.

      Apollo 1, 27 January 1967

      A flash fire swept the cabin during a launch-pad test with 100% oxygen. The crew of three died. NASA restricted combustible materials and replaced the three-piece hatch with one that opens in three seconds.

      NASA Apollo 1 · SMA

      Apollo 13, April 1970

      An oxygen tank exploded on the way to the Moon after heater damage. Wiring insulation added after Apollo 1 helped prevent arcing when the Command Module was powered up again.

      NASA Apollo 13 · SMA

      Mir, 24 February 1997

      A solid-fuel oxygen generator canister burst into flames, blocking the route to a Soyuz. The crew of six fought it with extinguishers. Investigators named hydrocarbon contamination, likely latex glove material, as the probable cause.

      NASA History · Mir fire

      Sources, method and disclosure

      How scores work. Each of the 24 records has a 0–5 rating on seven criteria. The ratings are our editorial judgment from the PSI record text, the NASA guide and the challenge brief. They are not values published by NASA. Score = weighted average of the ratings, scaled to 100. Short descriptions are our paraphrase. The full record text, DOIs, publication lists and experiment run tables were pulled from the PSI repository on 14 November 2026, and the “Tested in PSI run tables” ranges are computed from those tables. Two ACME BRE entries that read 3400% and 2100% are treated as 34% and 21%. Cite each investigation by its DOI, as PSI asks.

      How the coverage map and gap matrix work. The page embeds every row of the PSI run tables and counts in the browser; no count in those sections is typed in. Inverse-flame rows are excluded from anything about chamber oxygen, because their oxygen is the burner stream. FLEX-2 rows are test-matrix combinations, so they are drawn as open circles and never added to run counts. ACME daily summaries are drawn as squares. Rows with oxygen but no pressure sit in the side rail. Rows with no oxygen cannot be drawn and are listed under the chart. A run is “near” an environment when its oxygen is within ±3 percentage points of the environment's oxygen range and its pressure is within ±20% of its pressure range. The hazard-to-investigation mapping is shown under the matrix and is our judgment. ACME E-FIELD Flames appears in the partial-gravity column as “analog only”, following the challenge brief, and is never counted as a PSI run. Two lunar definitions are kept apart by name: the “brief's region” (30% O₂ or greater at 10.2 psi or less, open-ended) and the “NASA lunar target” (the 30–34% O₂, 8.2–10.2 psia box that the matrix matches against). Risk = data gap × hazard weight × isolation, with the weights shown beside the ranked list. Only environments whose values are all cited can set a gap's rank; an environment with an assumed value is reported as a note.

      Three kinds of content, labeled throughout. PSI data comes from the PSI run tables and records. NASA literature (not in PSI) covers Saffire IV–VI, SoFIE-GEL and LUCI points, taken from the papers listed below and drawn as diamonds. AI-written covers the environment briefings, the gap wording, the hazard weights and the paraphrases of NASA text.

      Target environments. ISS, Orion, lunar and Mars transit values are from the NASA sources linked in the environment table. The Mars surface oxygen level is assumed and marked as such. Pressures were converted with 1 atm = 14.696 psi = 101.325 kPa.
